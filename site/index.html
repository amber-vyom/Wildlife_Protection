How it works

From your report to the animal's release, every step is tracked.

1

Report

Tell us where the animal is and what you see. Add a photo or map location if you can.

2

Rescue

A coordinator sends a field agent, who captures the animal and brings it to the centre.

3

Treat

Vets diagnose and treat it. Caretakers feed, monitor and log its recovery every day.

4

Release

With Forest Department permission, healthy animals go back to the wild and can be tracked.

Before you report: keep a safe distance, don't feed or handle the animal, and keep pets and people away. If someone is in danger, call your local emergency number first.

What to do when you find an injured animal

Your first few minutes matter, but so does your own safety.

Read more
  • Keep a safe distance. Injured animals are frightened and may bite, scratch or kick.
  • Keep people, children and pets away, and keep noise low.
  • Do not feed or give water unless a rescuer tells you to.
  • Note the exact location, the species if you know it, and what the injury looks like. A photo helps.
  • Report it right away and keep your tracking code.

Safety

Snakes, monkeys and large animals: stay back

Some rescues need trained people and special equipment.

Read more

Never try to catch, move or corner a snake, a monkey, a large mammal or a bird of prey yourself. Most bites and injuries happen during untrained rescue attempts. Stay at a distance, keep watching from a safe place, and give rescuers a clear description of where the animal is and which way it moved. If the animal is on a road, warn traffic from a safe spot rather than approaching it.

Birds

Is that baby bird really abandoned?

Many young birds on the ground are fine and their parents are nearby.

Read more

Fledglings often leave the nest before they can fly well and spend a few days on the ground while their parents keep feeding them. Leave them alone unless they are visibly injured, bleeding, covered in insects or in immediate danger from traffic or cats. A fully feathered bird that hops and flaps is usually a healthy fledgling. A bird with few or no feathers that has fallen from a nest may need help, so report it.

Behind the scenes

From rescue to release: the journey of a rescued animal

What happens after the animal reaches the centre.

Read more

After capture, the animal is admitted and placed in a suitable enclosure, in quarantine if needed. A veterinarian examines it, records a diagnosis and writes a treatment plan, and surgery is scheduled if required. Caretakers feed it, log its weight, temperature and behaviour every day, and alert the vet if something looks wrong. When the vet judges it fit, the centre applies for Forest Department permission. Only then is the animal released, or moved to a sanctuary if it can't return to the wild.

Five ways to help without touching an animal

You can support wildlife rescue even if you never find an injured animal.

Read more
  • Volunteer for shifts such as cleaning, food preparation or transport support.
  • Donate money, or supplies such as bandages and food.

Law

Why release needs Forest Department permission

Wild animals are protected, and where they go back matters.

Read more

Wildlife is protected by law, so a rescue centre cannot simply release an animal wherever it likes. The Forest Department checks that the animal can be released into a suitable habitat of its own species, away from conflict with people. Our system records the permit number against each release, and an animal cannot be marked as released without one.

It was designed with use cases, a class diagram and role-based access, then built as a working web app.

Questions

Do I need an account to report an animal?

No. Reporting is open to everyone and no personal details are stored. You get a tracking code to check progress.

How do I follow my report?

Open the Track page and enter the 8-character code you received after submitting.

Who can create an account?

Anyone can sign up as a donor or volunteer. Staff accounts are created by the centre's coordinator.

Is my data safe?

Passwords are stored as salted hashes, accounts lock after repeated failed sign-ins, and sessions time out when idle.

What happens to the animal afterwards?

Once the vet clears it and the Forest Department grants a permit, it is released or moved to a sanctuary. Released animals can be tracked.
